# THE SHIT SHOW WITH CONFETTI

## Crisis-remediation economies, burden transfer, institutional self-preservation, racial hierarchy, moral disengagement, and why humane success must be structurally rewarded

**Mya P. Brown**  
Founder & Principal Architect, The Commons Initiative  
Working Paper v1.0 • September 2026

> **The confetti is the language of success. The ground is the result. If the human condition underneath has not improved, more confetti is not reform. It is decoration.**

## Abstract
This paper develops a systems-level critique of what it calls the **“Shit Show with Confetti”**: a social paradigm in which institutions publicly celebrate service, equity, care, progress, and accountability while large portions of the population continue to absorb predictable harms created or intensified upstream. The central claim is not that every worker, public servant, nonprofit, or human being is malicious. It is that harmful outcomes can persist when organizational incentives reward activity, throughput, liability avoidance, status preservation, and crisis management more reliably than they reward prevention, correction, dignity, or the disappearance of the problem itself.

The paper synthesizes organizational psychology, public administration, civil-rights enforcement, homelessness-policy research, and historical evidence. It proposes several Commons Initiative constructs: the **Remediation Economy**, **Cruelty Premium**, **Cowardice Dividend**, **Success-Transition Principle**, **Burden Follows Power**, **Reality Outranks Institutional Self-Protection**, and **Harm Interrupts Authority**.

## 1. The artifact: a shit show with confetti
Imagine the present institutional paradigm as a public square. From a distance it is gorgeous. Bright banners read **EQUITY, CARE, HOUSING, SAFETY, RECOVERY, COMMUNITY, OPPORTUNITY, JUSTICE**. Confetti cannons fire. Annual reports gleam. Mission statements promise dignity.

Then the viewer walks closer.

Under the confetti are unresolved housing crises, fragmented programs, bad accounting, impossible paperwork, repeated appointments, inaccessible services, racialized discretion, untreated disability needs, poverty penalties, preventable homelessness, and people spending extraordinary portions of their lives proving facts that should already be visible to the institutions controlling their lives.

The analytical question is not merely, “Are there good people?” It is: **What does the architecture amplify?** Harm does not need numerical superiority if it possesses greater leverage.

## 2. Why the bad guy runs
A person who commits a harmful act and then flees illustrates a simple principle: the benefit of the act was desired, while the cost of accountability was not. Institutions can reproduce the same pattern without physically running anywhere.

Institutional equivalents include: **“That was policy.” “Another department handled it.” “The system calculated it.” “We followed procedure.”** These statements can be relevant, but they can also move responsibility away from the people who exercised authority.

Albert Bandura’s work on **moral disengagement** describes mechanisms such as displacement and diffusion of responsibility, euphemistic labeling, minimizing consequences, and blaming or dehumanizing victims. Organizational research has linked moral disengagement to unethical workplace conduct.

This paper calls the resulting benefit the **Cowardice Dividend**: the advantage obtained when authority is exercised but responsibility for consequences is displaced upward, downward, sideways, or outward.

## 3. The Remediation Economy
Society devotes enormous resources to managing consequences created upstream. Homelessness produces shelters, outreach systems, emergency medicine, encampment response, legal services, case management, benefits navigation, transitional programs, and court proceedings. Administrative error produces grievance systems and appeals. Discrimination produces civil-rights investigations. Addiction produces treatment networks and monitoring systems.

These downstream services are often lifesaving. The critique is not that rescue is bad. The critique is that rescue can become more institutionally durable than prevention.

The U.S. Government Accountability Office has documented fragmentation and overlap in federal homelessness programs and warned that administrative requirements can burden providers and people seeking help. When funding and institutional survival are tied primarily to the continuing existence of crisis, a governance question appears: **what happens when success threatens the organization’s client base, budget, or institutional importance?**

## 4. Success must be organizationally survivable
The Commons Initiative proposes the **Success-Transition Principle**:

> An institution should never become financially safer when the harm it exists to reduce grows, and financially endangered merely because the harm declines.

A fire department is not rewarded for producing more fires. Its mission includes prevention, readiness, inspection, education, and rapid response. Social-service systems should be designed similarly.

If homelessness falls, resources should transition toward tenancy preservation, eviction prevention, affordable-housing supply, disability support, and emergency readiness. If recovery becomes durable, treatment funding should not depend on unnecessary repeat interventions. If a housing authority resolves disputes early and accurately, it should not be disadvantaged because enforcement volume falls.

## 5. Motion is not progress
Beds filled, tests billed, notices issued, contacts made, forms processed, and cases closed are easy to count. They become dangerous when they replace the outcome the service exists to produce.

A person can interact with fifteen programs and remain homeless. Every organization can record a successful contact while no one records the central failure: **the person still lacks a home.**

A society can therefore be extremely busy and still fail.

## 6. Ferguson: when public service becomes revenue extraction
The U.S. Department of Justice investigation of Ferguson, Missouri documented a public system whose incentives were distorted toward revenue generation. DOJ concluded that police and municipal court practices violated constitutional rights, were unduly focused on revenue, reflected and exacerbated racial bias, and disproportionately harmed Black residents.

Ferguson proves a crucial point: **public institutions can create financial incentives that make punitive contact with vulnerable residents institutionally useful.** That mechanism is real and must be designed against.

## 7. Greenwood and Black Wall Street: when equality becomes a threat
The Justice Department’s 2025 review of the 1921 Tulsa Race Massacre concluded that the destruction of Greenwood was not adequately described as spontaneous mob disorder. White residents and law-enforcement actors coordinated a systematic attack on a thriving Black community.

DOJ noted that armed Black men attempting to prevent a lynching were perceived as an unacceptable challenge to the social order. This matters because hierarchy can punish not only deprivation but **unauthorized advancement**. Prosperity, independence, political organization, self-defense, and legal assertion can be experienced as threats when equality is interpreted as loss of dominant status.

## 8. The ordinary manager problem
The hardest governance problem is not the obvious villain. It is the ordinary career-minded manager.

Put an ordinary person in an organization. They need income. They want stability. They learn who receives praise, who receives promotions, who becomes “difficult,” and which truths leadership prefers not to hear.

Research on **unethical pro-organizational behavior** shows that employees can engage in wrongdoing intended to benefit the organization, and that leaders under some conditions may reward or trust such behavior. Research on whistleblowing and abusive supervision shows that speaking up can carry organizational costs.

The ethical employee can become dangerous because they demonstrate that another choice was possible.

## 9. Revealed institutional preference
Institutions do not possess one nervous system, so claims that an organization emotionally “enjoys” harm require caution. But repeated choices can reveal preference.

If an organization knows an arrangement produces harm, knows it is expensive, knows feasible alternatives exist, possesses authority to change, and repeatedly preserves the arrangement, ignorance becomes progressively less persuasive.

The protected value may be revenue, discretion, workload simplicity, political control, career security, hierarchy, legal positioning, avoidance of precedent, or avoidance of admitting error.

## 10. The Cruelty Premium
The **Cruelty Premium** is the organizational advantage obtained when harmful practice produces compliance, revenue, deterrence, control, workload reduction, hierarchy preservation, or another benefit while the human costs are borne primarily outside the organization.

The bad option can be socially irrational while remaining locally rewarded.

The resident pays in time. The child pays in distress. The worker pays in lost wages. The public pays in emergency services. Another department pays legal expenses later. A future administration performs reform. The original decision-maker may still preserve status, authority, and favorable performance statistics.

## 11. Burden transfer
One of the ugliest features of administrative failure is the transfer of corrective labor onto the person affected.

The resident must detect the error, request records, compare numbers, document dates, explain disability effects, preserve evidence, locate counsel, understand appeals, and remain composed enough to avoid being recast as the problem.

The institution created the decision, possesses the records, employs specialists, and controls the process. Yet the weaker party performs the integration work.

Commons rejects this inversion through **Burden Follows Power**:

> The greater an actor’s capacity to affect another person’s housing, liberty, employment, healthcare, education, safety, or survival, the greater that actor’s duty to verify, explain, document, justify, correct, repair, and make appeal practicable.

## 12. Racialized administrative burden
Administrative burden is not distributed evenly. Money can buy counsel. Flexible employment can buy time. Language fluency can reduce interpretation costs. Transportation can reduce access barriers. And racial stereotypes can make ordinary anger or assertiveness carry different risks for different people.

Formal procedural equality does not guarantee equal lived burden.

## 13. The bad-apple defense is not enough
Sometimes misconduct is isolated. Sometimes one employee violates a healthy system and is properly disciplined.

But removing one person does not prove the organization is healthy. The inquiry must continue: who hired, supervised, rewarded, ignored, knew, objected, benefited, and had authority to interrupt? Did the misconduct help meet an organizational target? Did leadership change the incentive or merely replace the employee?

Accountability must follow **authority, benefit, knowledge, and failure to interrupt**.

## 14. The apple on the foot
The apple metaphor is intentionally simple.

Throw an apple upward and gravity returns it to the ground. Once it hits your foot, an institution cannot change reality by continuing to stare upward and claiming the fall remains unresolved.

**Reality Outranks Institutional Self-Protection.**

A ledger that does not reconcile must be reconciled. A harmful method must be reconsidered when its consequences are known. A decision resting on a factual contradiction must be reopened.

The system must look down when the apple lands.

## 15. Harm interrupts authority
Punishment after the fact is insufficient. Governments and corporations can pay settlements, shift budgets, insure losses, and move personnel.

Commons therefore proposes:

> **Harm interrupts authority.**

When credible evidence shows that an exercise of power is producing serious harm or rests upon a material unresolved contradiction, discretionary authority should shrink until independent review occurs.

The goal is not perfect people. It is a system that does not give imperfect people catastrophic reach.

## 16. The Commons alternative
Commons is not utopia. It is a **floor**.

Food, shelter, basic healthcare, education, information, physical safety, due process, the ability to understand decisions, and the ability to challenge authority cannot be converted into discretionary weapons.

Above that floor, people may compete, invent, own businesses, earn wealth, disagree, take risks, create art, fail, and try again.

The floor contains circuit breakers:

- bad accounting → freeze coercive enforcement;
- known harm → change the method;
- complaint against an employee → independent review;
- new evidence → reopen the decision;
- abuse of authority → shrink authority;
- error → correct and repair;
- successful reduction of crisis → transition resources upstream into prevention.

## 17. Conclusion: the confetti does not clean it up
The “Shit Show with Confetti” does not claim that every public servant is corrupt, every nonprofit is fraudulent, every intervention is useless, or every human being is cruel.

It is a claim about **aggregate design**.

A civilization may sincerely celebrate care while repeatedly funding downstream management instead of upstream prevention. It may employ wonderful rescuers while continuously manufacturing emergencies for them to rescue. It may measure activity more easily than resolution. It may protect organizational continuity more reliably than human dignity. It may externalize the cost of error onto the people least able to absorb it.

The confetti is the language of success.

The ground is the result.

If the answer underneath is still homelessness, instability, humiliation, administrative exhaustion, racialized burden, preventable harm, and people spending their lives correcting institutions that possess more power and information than they do, **more confetti is not reform. It is decoration.**

The world does not need a prettier rescue machine.

**It needs fewer trapdoors.**
